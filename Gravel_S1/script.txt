Somewhere in your house there is a succulent sitting on a layer of gravel. The person who
potted it was being careful. They had heard that gravel at the bottom helps water drain. So
they built the pot the way almost every gardening article says to. Here is what is actually
happening inside that pot. And here is why that layer of rocks is doing the opposite of what
it promises.

Every pot keeps a wet zone at the bottom after you water it. This is true no matter how many
holes the pot has. The water stops dripping out and stays there. It is held by capillary
tension. That is the same force that pulls water up the edge of a glass. It is also the force
that pulls water up the fibres of a paper towel. That saturated zone at the bottom of your
pot is called the perched water table. Here is the part that surprises most people. Its
height is decided by the particle size of your soil. It is not decided by your pot. It is
not decided by your drainage holes. Fine powdery soil holds a tall saturated zone. Coarse
gritty soil holds a short one. Think of a wet sponge. Lift it and it stops dripping. The
bottom still stays thoroughly soaked. Cutting more holes in the sponge changes nothing.
Using a coarser sponge changes everything.

And the bag of soil that made your pothos lush is the same bag that can drown your
succulent. General-purpose mix is engineered to hold water. Most houseplants want that.
Your succulent evolved in the opposite conditions. So the problem is never that you watered
wrong once. It is that the soil around the roots stays wet long after the watering is over.

Why does particle size matter so much? Because capillary tension works through the tiny
spaces between grains. In fine soil those spaces are narrow. Water climbs high and stays
trapped there. In coarse soil the spaces are wide. Water falls through them instead of
clinging. The result is a gritty mix that drains by gravity and then holds almost nothing.
A peat mix holds a tall wet layer even after it has stopped dripping. This is not a small
difference. It is the difference between a root zone that breathes and a root zone that is
underwater for days after every watering.

That matters because roots need oxygen. They do not just need water. Root cells respire.
They do it the same way your muscles do. The air held in the spaces between soil particles
is what feeds them after drainage. A waterlogged root zone cuts that oxygen supply. A
succulent is especially vulnerable to that. Most succulents use a type of photosynthesis
that makes them very slow at drying their own pot. A thirsty tropical plant will pump water
out of wet soil in days. It rescues itself. A succulent in the same wet soil will not. Its
greatest survival trick is its ability to hoard water. That same ability is exactly why the
wet zone can sit there for weeks. The soil is not wrong on its own. The plant is not weak
on its own. The combination is what kills.

So when a succulent sits in wet soil, the instinct is to add a layer of gravel at the
bottom. The goal is to help the water escape. Here is the surprise. Water does not move out
of fine soil into a coarse gravel layer. At least it does not move the way intuition
expects. At the boundary between the two layers, the water in the soil is held by capillary
tension. It will not cross into the gravel until the soil above it is completely saturated.
That boundary is called a textural discontinuity. The practical meaning is simple. The
gravel does not drain your soil. It parks the wet zone right above the gravel line. That is
closer to the roots than the bottom of the pot was. You have moved the problem up an inch.
You have also given away root space. Every centimetre of gravel is a centimetre your roots
could have used. And if the gravel reaches the drainage hole, it can even act as a little
reservoir. It holds water against the bottom of the soil plug. It is like a saucer you
cannot see.

Where did this advice come from? It survives because of very large outdoor planters. In a
deep soil column the wet zone is a small fraction of the whole. So nobody notices the harm.
And gravel there is really about weight and saving soil. It is not about drainage. In a
small indoor succulent pot, none of that applies. The layer does not lift the wet zone out
of reach. It lifts it into reach.

How do you know if your pot has this problem? First look at the bottom of the pot. If you
see a band of rocks under the soil, you have a drainage layer. Everything above that band
is where the water collects. Second feel the soil just above that line a day after
watering. If it stays cold and wet while the top of the pot is dry, the perched water table
is sitting exactly where the roots are. That is the whole diagnostic. Find the layer. Then
feel what is sitting on top of it. The plants will show you the rest. The fastest
punishers are always the same three.

Here is the whole decision in one line. Do not build a drainage layer under your soil.
Build the drainage into the soil itself. A gritty predominantly mineral mix works best.
Pumice, perlite, or coarse grit all work. Larger particles hold a shorter saturated zone.
That is why the perched water table shrinks to almost nothing. That is the same reason
these plants grow in their natural habitat in loose mineral ground. They do not grow in a
bag of wet sponge. One real drainage hole at the bottom is enough. No standing water in the
saucer. And a mix that dries out fully between waterings. That is the fix. It is the same
fix for every succulent on this channel. The physics of water in a pot does not care which
rosette you are growing.

Here is how you know the mix is right. Water it thoroughly. Then wait until the pot feels
noticeably lighter before you water again. That lightness is the dry-down. It is the single
most reliable signal a succulent owner has. If the pot still feels heavy after a week, the
mix is holding water. No drainage layer underneath is going to fix that.

There is a quieter version of this same mistake. It hides in plain sight. A decorative pot
without a drainage hole often holds a nursery pot inside it. That setup recreates the
perched water table above the cachepot's own base. Water that drains from the inner pot
collects in that hidden gap. It sits against the soil. Same physics. Same result. And it is
one of the most common setups in any home.

Picture three beginners. The first bought a String of Pearls. She added gravel when she
repotted. Now the strands near the soil line are shrivelling. She waters more because
shrivelled pearls look thirsty. That is the failure loop. The fix is not more water. It is
a grittier mix and a full dry-down between waterings. The second has an Echeveria elegans.
It looks healthy but has not grown in months. No rot. No drama. Just nothing. Its roots are
sitting in the wet band above the gravel. They are slowly losing their oxygen supply. When
he repots into gritty mix, the plant usually starts growing again. The third has a Lithops.
It went soft and translucent after one heavy watering. The gravel layer did not save it. A
gritty mix might have.

After you repot, do not expect a miracle by the weekend. Recovery is slow. Roots take time
to regrow into new soil. The plant has to spend its stored energy building them before it
can push out new leaves. What changes is the direction of travel. Instead of sitting in a
wet band losing air, the roots are finally working in the conditions they evolved for. Give
the plant a full dry-down. Then water thoroughly again. Let the mix do the draining. That
is the whole system. The pot does not need rocks. It needs a sponge that dries out.

So the next time you are tempted to add a layer of gravel, remember the sponge. More holes
never fixed a wet sponge. A towel underneath never dried it. What changed the sponge was
the sponge itself. Your succulent does not need rocks at the bottom. It needs soil that
drains the way its home does.
