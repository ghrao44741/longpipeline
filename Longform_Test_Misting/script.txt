Succulents are vascular plants, which means they have an internal water-transport system — the xylem — that works a bit like a series of drinking straws running from the roots to the tips of the leaves.

Water enters through root hairs — tiny, thread-like structures at the very tips of the roots. Those root hairs are deep in the soil, pressed against moist particles, pulling water in through osmosis — the same principle as a tea bag drawing liquid in when it's submerged.

Leaves can absorb small amounts of moisture through their outer waxy coating — called the cuticle — but it's minimal. Not enough to sustain the plant. Not what succulents rely on.

So when you mist, you're watering the leaves and the surface of the soil. But the root hairs — the actual drinking organs — are sitting down in the lower zone of the pot, dry, waiting.

The water never reaches them.

Here is what is actually happening in the pot when you mist.

The top half-inch to inch of soil absorbs the water. It darkens. It looks like you have watered. But water only moves downward by gravity when there is enough of it to saturate the upper layers and flow through. A fine mist rarely delivers that volume.

So the moisture sits near the surface. It does not reach the root zone.

Meanwhile, that surface stays damp — sometimes for hours, sometimes longer indoors. And damp surfaces are exactly where fungal spores thrive. Fungi like Fusarium — which works a bit like mold on bread — need persistent surface moisture to germinate and spread.

Succulents use a special kind of photosynthesis called CAM — Crassulacean Acid Metabolism. Instead of opening their pores during the day like most plants, succulents open their stomata at night, when it is cooler and they lose less water to evaporation.

This is one of the reasons succulents handle dry conditions so well — they are operating on a different schedule. But it also means that even the small amount of foliar moisture absorption that can happen through open stomata is most likely to occur at night. Misting in the morning, which is when most people do it, is spraying water onto closed pores.
